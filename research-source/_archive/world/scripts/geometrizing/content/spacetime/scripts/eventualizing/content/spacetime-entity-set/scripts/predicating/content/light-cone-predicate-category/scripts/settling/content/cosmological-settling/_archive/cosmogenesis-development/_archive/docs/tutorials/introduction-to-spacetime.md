# Introduction to Spacetime

When we first learn physics, spacetime is usually described as a stage.
It is the four-dimensional setting—three dimensions of space and one of time—in which all events occur.
A ball falls, a planet orbits, two particles collide, and each of these events happens at some place and some moment.
On this view, spacetime is a fixed background, already there, waiting for things to happen inside it.

This course takes a different starting point.
Here, spacetime is not a fixed background but a process.
It is something built up in stages out of more basic ingredients.
The most basic ingredient is information, and the stages by which information becomes the familiar world of space, time, matter, and heat are called the four regimes of spacetime.
The four regimes are, in order, the informational, the quantum, the classical, and the thermal.
The goal of this chapter is to introduce them and to show how each one grows out of the one before it.
We will build them up one at a time, starting from the simplest possible thing.

The simplest thing in physics is a distinction: a difference between two possibilities.
A light switch is either on or off.
A coin lands either heads or tails.
A single yes-or-no answer separates one option from the other.
We call such a two-way distinction a bit.
The bit is the basic unit of information, and in this course it is also the basic unit of spacetime.
Before there is any space, any time, or any matter, there is only the capacity to tell one thing from another.
This first stage is called informational spacetime, because here the world is made of distinctions and nothing else.

A bit by itself is only a possibility: on or off, with neither yet chosen.
Two basic operations act on it.
The first fixes its value—it makes the bit definitely on or definitely off and records or sends that value as a signal.
The second erases the value, returning the bit to an undecided state.
Erasing loses information, because once a bit is blank we can no longer tell what it had been.
These two operations, fixing and erasing, are enough to describe how information is stored and transmitted, which is why ordinary information technology—telephones, computers, the internet—can be understood entirely at this level.

But there is something a bit cannot carry by itself.
A signal can tell you whether a switch is on or off, but it cannot tell you what the switch is for.
The value of a bit can be sent; the meaning of the bit—what the distinction is about—cannot be sent in the same way.
This leftover meaning is not a flaw, and it is not lost.
It is the raw material from which the next regime is built.
When the leftover meanings of many bits are gathered together instead of discarded, the world takes its next step.

That step produces the second regime, quantum spacetime.
Its basic object is not a definite fact but a possibility held open, which physicists call a superposition.
A good example is a single particle, such as an electron, before it is measured.
An electron has a property called spin, which a measurement always reports as either "up" or "down."
Before the measurement, however, the electron need not be either; it can be in a combination of both at once.
This is not a case of the electron secretly being up or down while we remain ignorant.
As far as any experiment can tell, the definite value does not exist yet.
The electron is in superposition.

Particles in this regime have a second important feature: identical particles can share the same state.
Many photons in a laser beam, for instance, occupy the same state and act together as one coherent thing.
A shared state of this kind is called a mode, and much of quantum physics is the study of how identical particles fill modes.
The holding-open of these undecided possibilities is called coherence.
Coherence is delicate: it lasts only as long as nothing forces a definite value.

What forces a definite value is measurement.
When we measure the electron's spin we get a definite answer, up or down, and the superposition is gone.
Measurement is the operation that turns an open possibility into a settled fact.
It is also the bridge to the next regime, because a world of settled facts is the ordinary world we live in.

The third regime is classical spacetime.
This is the familiar world of definite objects with definite positions, speeds, and histories: a ball on a table, a car on a road, a planet on its orbit.
Everything here has a definite value, because the quantum possibilities have been settled by measurement.
Most of everyday physics, and most of astronomy, takes place in this regime.

The classical regime is governed by two opposite tendencies, and much of cosmology is the competition between them.
The first tendency gathers: it pulls things together and holds them in place.
We see it as gravity, the attraction of matter for matter, which draws clumps of matter into denser clumps.
The second tendency disperses: it pushes things apart and spreads them out.
We see it as the expansion of the universe, driven by what astronomers call dark energy.
Gravity and dark energy are not two unrelated puzzles.
They are the two basic tendencies of this regime, one gathering and one dispersing.

The balance between them shapes the history of the universe.
Early on, gravity is the stronger of the two, and the expansion slows down.
Later, the dispersing tendency takes over, and the expansion speeds up.
Structure forms wherever gravity wins locally: a galaxy, a star cluster, or a black hole is a region where matter has been pulled tightly together while the universe as a whole keeps expanding.
A black hole is the most extreme example, a place where matter has collapsed about as far as it can.
A void—a vast nearly empty region between galaxies—is the opposite, a place where the dispersing tendency has won and almost nothing has gathered.
Because the classical regime is the only one with definite values, it is also the only one we can measure directly.
The measurement of the universe at large scale—how fast it expands, what it is made of, how its structures formed—is called cosmology, and it belongs to this regime.

The fourth and final regime is thermal spacetime.
It describes what happens to a collection of definite objects over long times, as they settle toward a shared state.
A hot cup of coffee left on a table cools until it matches the temperature of the room.
A drop of ink in water spreads until the color is uniform.
These are not facts about any single particle; they are facts about a whole collection coming into balance.
The physics of such balance—temperature, heat, and the quantity called entropy that measures how spread-out and settled a system is—is thermodynamics, and thermodynamics is the natural language of this regime.

The thermal regime explains something the earlier regimes do not: the direction of time.
The basic laws that govern single particles work equally well forwards and backwards.
A film of two particles colliding looks sensible played either way.
But a film of coffee cooling, or of ink spreading, looks obviously wrong when run in reverse.
The difference is that cooling and spreading describe a whole system settling toward balance, and that settling goes only one way.
This one-way settling is the origin of the arrow of time.
Time has a direction not because the underlying laws prefer one, but because large collections of things settle toward balance and never spontaneously away from it.

Carried to its conclusion for the universe as a whole, this settling ends in a state called heat death.
In heat death every temperature is equal, every difference has been evened out, and nothing is left to change.
It is completely settled and completely still.
Such a state contains no observers.
Living things, measuring instruments, and any process that records or computes all depend on differences and on change, and a fully settled universe has neither.
This is why we never observe heat death directly: we always find ourselves partway through the settling, in a universe that is still changing—which is exactly the condition required for anyone to be present to measure it.

Putting the four regimes together gives a single picture.
The world begins as pure information: bare distinctions, bits with no definite values.
Gathering the leftover meaning of those bits produces the quantum regime, a world of possibilities held open in superposition.
Measurement settles those possibilities into the definite objects of the classical regime—the ordinary world of matter, gravity, and the expanding universe.
Over long times, those definite objects settle toward balance in the thermal regime, whose endpoint is heat death.

For a first course, the most important point is that spacetime, in this framework, is not a fixed container that exists before everything else.
It is the layered result of information settling into the world, one regime at a time.
Each regime has its own objects and its own rules: distinctions in the informational regime, superpositions and modes in the quantum regime, definite bodies and gravity in the classical regime, temperature and entropy in the thermal regime.
Each grows out of the one before it through a definite step—gathering, measuring, settling.
And the familiar space and time of everyday life appear in the middle of this sequence, in the classical regime, not at its beginning.
The chapters that follow take up each regime in turn, starting with information and the bit.
