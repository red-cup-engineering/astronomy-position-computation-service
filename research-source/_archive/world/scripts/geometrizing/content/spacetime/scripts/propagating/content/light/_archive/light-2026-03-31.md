Light is electromagnetic radiation: oscillating electric and magnetic fields that propagate through space at a fixed speed, approximately 300,000 kilometers per second in vacuum. This speed -- denoted $c$ -- is the fastest that any signal, particle, or information can travel.

Light is the primary messenger of cosmology. Nearly everything we know about the universe beyond our solar system arrives as electromagnetic radiation collected by telescopes. Because light travels at finite speed, observing a distant object means observing it as it was when the light left it. A galaxy ten billion light-years away is seen as it was ten billion years ago. Looking far away is looking back in time.

Light can be described as a wave (with a [wavelength](./electromagnetic-spectrum.md) and frequency) or as a stream of particles (photons). Both descriptions are correct and useful in different contexts. The [cosmic microwave background](theories/structure/terms/cosmic-microwave-background.md) is light emitted 380,000 years after the Big Bang, now stretched by the expansion of space to microwave wavelengths. The [redshift](./redshift.md) of light from distant galaxies is the principal evidence that the universe is expanding.
