General relativity is Einstein's theory of gravity (1915), in which the geometry of [spacetime](./spacetime.md) is determined by the distribution of mass and energy, and the geometry of spacetime in turn determines how matter moves.

The Einstein field equations relate two mathematical objects: the Einstein tensor $G_{\mu\nu}$ (encoding spacetime curvature) and the stress-energy tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$ (encoding the density and flow of mass-energy):

$$G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu}$$

The constant $\Lambda$ is the [cosmological constant](theories/structure/terms/dark-energy.md), which Einstein originally introduced and later abandoned, and which was revived in 1998 when the [accelerating expansion](theories/evolution/terms/accelerating-expansion.md) was discovered.

General relativity is the foundation of modern cosmology. Applied to a universe that is homogeneous and isotropic on large scales (the [cosmological principle](./cosmological-principle.md)), it yields the [Friedmann equations](theories/evolution/terms/friedmann-equations.md), which govern the expansion of the universe. Applied to the collapse of massive objects, it predicts [black holes](theories/black-holes/terms/black-hole.md). Applied to the bending of light around massive structures, it predicts [gravitational lensing](theories/observation/terms/gravitational-lensing.md).

The theory's predictions have been confirmed by the perihelion precession of Mercury, the deflection of starlight by the Sun, gravitational redshift, gravitational waves (detected by LIGO in 2015), and the imaging of black hole shadows (Event Horizon Telescope, 2019).
