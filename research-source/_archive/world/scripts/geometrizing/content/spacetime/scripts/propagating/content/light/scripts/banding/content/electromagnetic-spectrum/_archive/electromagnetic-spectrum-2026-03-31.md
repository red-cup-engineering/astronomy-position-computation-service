The electromagnetic spectrum is the full range of electromagnetic radiation, from the longest radio waves to the shortest gamma rays. All electromagnetic radiation is the same physical phenomenon -- oscillating electric and magnetic fields propagating through space at the speed of [light](./light.md) -- differing only in wavelength and frequency.

The spectrum, from longest to shortest wavelength:

| Band | Wavelength | Cosmological relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Radio | > 1 mm | Radio galaxies, pulsars, 21-cm hydrogen line |
| Microwave | 1 mm - 1 m | [Cosmic microwave background](theories/structure/terms/cosmic-microwave-background.md) |
| Infrared | 700 nm - 1 mm | High-redshift galaxies, dust-obscured regions |
| Visible | 400 - 700 nm | Optical galaxy surveys, supernovae |
| Ultraviolet | 10 - 400 nm | Hot stars, active galactic nuclei |
| X-ray | 0.01 - 10 nm | Galaxy cluster gas, black hole accretion |
| Gamma ray | < 0.01 nm | Gamma-ray bursts, extreme particle acceleration |

Wavelength $\lambda$ and frequency $\nu$ are related by $c = \lambda \nu$. Shorter wavelength means higher frequency means higher energy per photon ($E = h\nu$, where $h$ is Planck's constant).

Cosmology uses the entire spectrum. The [cosmic microwave background](theories/structure/terms/cosmic-microwave-background.md) peaks in microwaves. Galaxy surveys operate in visible and infrared. [Redshift](./redshift.md) moves light along the spectrum: a galaxy whose light was emitted in the ultraviolet may be observed in the infrared if it is at high enough redshift. X-ray telescopes reveal the hot gas in [galaxy clusters](theories/structure/terms/galaxy-cluster.md). Radio telescopes map neutral hydrogen. No single band gives a complete picture; cosmology depends on observations across the entire electromagnetic spectrum.
